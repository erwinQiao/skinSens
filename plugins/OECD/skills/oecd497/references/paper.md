# 1 Section 1-Introduction

## 1.1. General Introduction

1. A skin sensitiser refers to a substance that will lead to an allergic response following repeated skin contact as defined by the United Nations Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (UN GHS) (1). There is general agreement on the key biological events underlying skin sensitisation. The current knowledge of the chemical and biological mechanisms associated with skin sensitisation initiated by covalent binding to proteins has been summarised as an Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) (2) that begins with a molecular initiating event, leading to intermediate key events (KEs), and terminating with the adverse effect, allergic contact dermatitis.

2. The skin sensitisation AOP focuses on chemicals that react with amino acid residues (*i.e.* cysteine or lysine) such as organic chemicals. In this instance, the molecular initiating event (*i.e.* the first KE), is the covalent binding of electrophilic substances to nucleophilic centres in skin proteins. The second KE in this AOP takes place in the keratinocytes and includes inflammatory responses as well as changes in gene expression associated with specific cell signalling pathways such as the antioxidant/electrophile response element (ARE)-dependent pathways. The third KE is the activation of dendritic cells, typically assessed by expression of specific cell surface markers, chemokines and cytokines. The fourth key event is T-cell proliferation, and the adverse outcome is presentation of allergic contact dermatitis.

3. The assessment of skin sensitisation has typically involved the use of laboratory animals. The classical methods that use guinea pigs, the Guinea Pig Maximisation Test (GPMT) and the Buehler Test (OECD TG 406) (3) assess both the induction and elicitation phases of skin sensitisation. The murine tests, such as the local lymph node assay (LLNA) (OECD TG 429) (4) and its three non-radioactive modifications — LLNA: DA (OECD TG 442A) (5), LLNA: BrdU-ELISA, and BrdU-FCM (OECD TG 442B) (6) — all assess the induction phase exclusively and have gained acceptance, since they provide an advantage over the guinea pig tests in terms of animal welfare together with an objective measurement of the induction phase of skin sensitisation.

4. Mechanistically-based _in chemico_ and _in vitro_ test methods (OECD TG 442C, 442D, 442E) (7, 8, 9) addressing the first three KEs of the skin sensitisation AOP can be used to evaluate the skin sensitisation hazard potential of chemicals. With the exception of the kinetic Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (k-DPRA) (TG442C), none of these test methods are considered sufficient stand-alone replacements of animal data to conclude on the skin sensitisation potential of chemicals or to provide information for potency sub-categorisation according to the UN GHS (sub-categories 1A and 1B). However, data generated with these _in chemico_ and _in vitro_ methods addressing multiple KEs of the skin sensitisation AOP can be used together, as well as with information sources such as _in silico_ and read-across predictions from chemical analogues, within integrated approaches to testing and assessment (IATA) or defined approaches (DAs). Results from the individual information sources can only be used in DAs if the substances fall within the applicability domains of the methods, which are described in the “Initial Considerations, Applicability and Limitations” sections of the respective methods (TG 442C, Appendices I-II; TG 442D, Appendices IA-IC; TG 442E Annexes 1-4) (7, 8, 9), and in Appendices I-V of this guideline for the _in silico_ information sources.

5. Results from multiple information sources can be used together in DAs to achieve an equivalent or better predictive capacity than that of the animal tests to predict responses in humans. A DA consists of a fixed data interpretation procedure (DIP) (*e.g.* a mathematical model, a rule-based approach) applied to data (*e.g.* *in silico* predictions, *in chemico*, *in vitro* data) generated with a defined set of information sources to derive a prediction without the need for expert judgment. Individual DAs for skin sensitisation and their respective information sources were originally described in Guidance Document 256, Annex I/II (10) and a preliminary assessment was published in Kleinstreuer et al (11). The DAs use method combinations intended to overcome some of the limitations of the individual methods to provide increased confidence in the overall result obtained. The ultimate goal of DAs is to provide information that is equivalent to that provided by animal studies, _i.e._ information that can be used for hazard identification, potency (sub)categorisation, and/or a quantitative measure of sensitisation potency, used to derive a point of departure (PoD).

6. Testing laboratories should consider all relevant available information on the test chemical prior to conducting the studies as directed by a DA. All available information could include, for example, the identity and chemical structure of the test chemical and its physicochemical properties. Such information should be considered in order to determine whether the individual OECD test guideline methods and any _in silico_ information source used under a specific DA are applicable for the test chemical.

7. When performing a hazard evaluation, potency (sub)-categorisation, and/or PoD prediction based on the output from an _in vivo_ (e.g. LLNA) test, an _in chemico_ test, an _in vitro_ test, an _in silico_ approach, a DA, or any combination thereof, the same principles always apply. That is, all available information relevant to the chemical in question as well as toxicological data on structurally related test chemicals, if available, should be taken into consideration.

8. This Guideline was developed with the input of an OECD Expert Group on Defined Approaches for Skin Sensitisation (EG DASS) comprised of scientific experts from regulatory agencies, validation bodies, non-governmental organisations, and industry.

9. Multiple rule-based DAs are included in this Guideline and are described in the context of their primary intended regulatory purpose: hazard identification, _i.e._ discrimination between skin sensitisers and non-sensitisers (Part I), potency sub-categorisation (Part II), or derivation of a point of departure (Part III)<sup>1</sup>. The DA included in Part II is also suitable for hazard identification (Part I). The DA(s) included in Part III cannot be used in Part I or Part II unless mentioned specifically for their use. The evaluation and review of the DAs are described in detail in the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation* (12).

10. A comprehensive dataset of 196 chemicals with DA predictions, data on individual information sources, highly curated LLNA and Human Patch Predictive Test (HPPT) data, and physicochemical properties, was compiled and is attached as **Annex 2** to the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (12). Information on the curation of this data is detailed in **Annexes 3** and **4** of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (12). Out of the 196 chemicals, 168 chemicals have classifications based on LLNA data and 66 chemicals have classifications based on human data, which were all agreed upon by the EG DASS and used to evaluate the performance of the DAs when using the initially included (see paragraph 11) test methods and _in silico_ models. Due to the availability of data, this dataset contains mainly cosmetic ingredients but also other types of chemicals that are used across sectors such as preservatives, dyes, or food ingredients. The dataset is chemically diverse as shown by the range of physicochemical properties covered by these chemicals: it contains small and large molecules (molecular weight ranges from 30 to

11. This Guideline, when initially adopted, included DAs developed to be performed with a specific set of methods, *i.e.* the Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (DPRA), KeratinoSens™, and the Human Cell Line Activation Test (h-CLAT), for the “2 out of 3” approach (2o3), and DPRA, h-CLAT, and either Derek Nexus or OECD quantitative structure-activity relationship ((Q)SAR) Toolbox for the integrated testing strategy (ITS). These DAs are referred as ‘classic DAs’ together or ‘classic 2o3 DA’ and ‘classic ITS DA’ throughout this Guideline. In the course of evaluating and developing the first DAs and specific information sources, a number of analyses were conducted by the EG DASS members. These include the drafting of an evaluation framework for each DA, building, curating, and characterising chemical reference lists of human and murine data, and developing decision trees and other tools to incorporate _in silico_ information sources into the relevant DAs and guideline. The group also identified sources of uncertainty and potential limitations of the DAs and information sources, as well as borderline test results, and assessed the impact of these on the performance of the DAs. These analyses are specific to the creation of the original DASS guideline and information sources published in 2021 and can be found in the main document and Annexes 1 & 3-8 of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation* (12).

12. In 2025, this guideline was updated to (i) include similar methods<sup>2</sup> to the methods (see paragraph 11) used for the initial adoption of this guideline and (ii) to include part III on Defined Approaches for derivation of PoD. Based on the 2025 revision, Annex 2 of the supporting document has been updated with new performance information relevant to the new information sources and DAs that have been added to the guideline.

13. Since the original guideline adoption, other _in chemico_ (included in TG 442C) and _in vitro_ (included in TGs 442D/E) methods have been proposed and inserted into this Guideline as alternative information sources in the DAs (see **Table 1.1** and subsequent sections of this document for the list of methods permitted for use in each DA). The performances of the DAs using the alternative information sources have been evaluated with available datasets for each permutation and can be found in **Tables 1.1-1.3** below and in **Annexes 2** and **9** of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (12).

15. The DAs described in this guideline are based on the use of validated OECD test methods for which transferability, within- and between-laboratory reproducibility have been characterised in the validation phase (OECD 442C/D/E, (7, 8, 9)).

16. The ITS DAs make use of well-defined _in silico_ information sources. Information on each model, including its description, applicability domain, and protocol to generate predictions is available in Appendices I-II. Additionally, key information related to the OECD Principles for the Validation, for Regulatory Purposes, of (Q)SAR Models (16), is described in individual QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) documents (17).

17. All DAs described in Parts I and II of this guideline can be used to address countries' requirements for discriminating sensitisers ( _i.e._ UN GHS Category 1) and non-sensitisers (UN GHS Not Classified (NC)), though they do so with different sensitivities and specificities (detailed in the respective descriptions of each DA and in **Annexes 2** and **9** of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation*) (12)).

18. The ITS DA can also be used to discriminate chemicals into three UN GHS potency categories (Category 1A = strong sensitisers; Category 1B = other sensitisers), and NC.

19. The known limitations and applicability domains of the individual information sources were used to design workflows for assigning confidence to each of the predictions produced by the DAs described in this guideline. In order to have a high confidence prediction, the underlying data must meet criteria in the respective _in chemico/in vitro_ TGs (see TG 442C, Appendices I-II; TG 442D, Appendices IA-IC; TG 442E Annexes 1-4 (7, 8, 9)); for the ITS DA, _in silico_ predictions must fall within the applicability domain of the model used (see Appendices I-II). DA predictions with high confidence for hazard identification and/or potency categorisation are considered conclusive. DA predictions with low confidence are considered inconclusive for hazard identification and/or potency categorisation (see **Sections 2.1.4** and **3.1.4** for further information). These inconclusive predictions may nevertheless be considered in a weight-of-evidence approach and/or within the context of an IATA together with other information sources (*e.g.* demonstration of exposure to the test system, existing _in vivo_ data, clinical data, read-across, other *in chemico/in vitro/in silico* data, etc.).

28. The SARA-ICE DA derives a point of departure for risk assessment. The model estimates the does at which skin sensitisation is likely to occur in 1% of an eligible human population (estimated dose, ED01), reported as a distribution with associated confidence intervals. An upper bound is imposed on ED01 values, at a threshold of 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup>. This reflects the bound for a GHS classification of NC and an implicit assumption that the chemical under consideration for PoD based risk assessment is a sensitiser at some exposure level. The PoD is therefore defined as the best estimate (geometric mean) of the distribution of the ED01 truncated at 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup>.

29. SARA-ICE PoD estimates were computed for the 196 chemicals in the defined approaches for skin sensitisation (DASS) reference data (12) using a) NAM data also reported in the evaluation set (single input for DPRA, KeratinoSens and h-CLAT) and b) a single kDPRA sourced from (Natsch and Gerberick, 2022). A DSA1+ MSPE value was defined for 54 / 66 chemicals with human reference data in the DASS evaluation set. A positive correlation exists (Pearson correlation coefficient is 0.64) and 46 / 54 (85%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value. Roughly one third, 19 / 54 (35%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. ### *1.3.2 Limitations of in silico information sources*

34. The ITS DA includes an _in silico_ tool as an information source. These tools can either perform automated read-across or (Q)SAR predictions. (Q)SARs include both structure-activity relationship (SAR) models (*i.e.* structural alerts, expert systems) and quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models (*i.e.* statistical tools). (Q)SAR models should fulfil the OECD Principles for the Validation, for Regulatory Purposes, of (Q)SAR Models and be described in a QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) document (16, 17)<sup>9</sup>. One of the OECD QSAR validation principles refers to a defined domain of applicability. The applicability domain is the response and chemical structure space in which the model makes predictions with a given reliability and the applicability domain should therefore consider the parametric, structural, mechanistic, metabolic and response space of the model. Where a DA for skin sensitisation includes an _in silico_ tool, users should refer to the limitations and applicability domain of the individual _in silico_ tool as detailed in **Appendices I-II** of this Guideline. Depending on the DIP, chemicals outside the _in silico_ model’s applicability domain may result in DA predictions of low confidence that are considered inconclusive.

### *1.3.3 Limitations of DAs*

For the 2o3 DA, BRs have been defined for the individual assays addressing the three KE of the DA, in order to define areas where lower confidence may exist (for details see **Section 2.1.4** and **Annex 1** of this Guideline, and **Section 3.3** and **Annex 7** of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation*) (12). Inconclusive predictions are not relevant for SARA-ICE for PoD determination as this is a continuous metric (not categorical) made upon the assumption a chemical is a sensitiser.

# Part I SECTION 2 - Defined Approaches for Skin Sensitisation Hazard Identification

38. Part I of this guideline applies to Defined Approaches (DAs) that are intended solely for hazard identification, _i.e._ distinguishing between sensitisers and non-sensitisers. A summary of the DAs for hazard identification is provided below; additional detailed information can be found in the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). Note that the ITS DAs can also be used for hazard characterization; please see **Part II** for more detail.

## **2.1 “2 out of 3” Defined Approach**

### **_2.1.1 Summary_**

39. The 2 out of 3 (2o3) DA is intended for the identification of the skin sensitisation hazard of a chemical (_i.e._ UN GHS Cat. 1 vs. UN GHS NC) without the use of animal testing, The data interpretation procedure (DIP) is currently not designed to provide information on the potency of a skin sensitiser.

40. The combination of test methods included in the 2o3 DA covers at least two of the first three KEs of the AOP leading to skin sensitisation, i.e. KE1: protein binding (OECD TG 442C) (2); KE2: keratinocyte activation (OECD TG 442D) (3); and KE3: dendritic cell activation (OECD TG 442E) (4).

41. The DIP requires that two concordant results obtained from methods addressing at least two of the first three KEs of the skin sensitisation AOP determine the final classification. For the classic 2o3 DA was evaluated against a curated dataset of up to168 chemicals with LLNA-based reference data and up to 66 chemicals with human-based reference data agreed upon by the EG DASS (**Annex 2** of the _Supporting Document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). The performance of the DAs using the original (i.e., classic DAs) and alternative information sources has been evaluated against both LLNA and human-based reference data, according to available datasets for each permutation, and can be found in **Tables 2.2-2.4** below and in **Annexes 2** and **9** of the _Supporting Document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). It should be noted that due to the imbalanced nature of the reference data (higher numbers of UN GHS Cat. 1 than UN GHS NC), the measures of balanced accuracy are more uncertain, particularly in the case of the human data comparison.

### **_2.1.2 Data interpretation procedure_**

42. The DIP in the 2o3 DA is a transparent, rule-based approach requiring no expert judgment (5, 6, 7). The approach predicts skin sensitisation hazard by sequential testing, in an undefined order, in up to three of the internationally accepted non-animal assays mapping to KE1-3. In other words, the assays are run for two KEs, and if these assays provide consistent results, then the chemical is predicted accordingly as skin sensitiser or non-sensitiser. If the first two assays provide discordant results, the assay for the remaining KE is run. The overall result is based on the two concordant findings considering the confidence of the obtained predictions as described in **Section 2.1.4**.

43. The performance of the 2o3 DA was found to be impacted by the consideration of BRs for each of the methods, as described below in **Section 2.1.4**, and further detailed in **Section 3.3** and **Annex 7** of the _Supporting Document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). A decision tree is provided in **Figure 2.1** of **Section 2.1.4** to derive predictions for the 2o3 DA, with no modification of the 2o3 DA DIP.

## **_2.1.3 Description and limitations of the individual information sources_**

44. The individual information sources in the DA are assays included in OECD KE- based TGs for skin sensitisation (OECD TG 442C, 442D, 442E) (2, 3, 4), and the details for the individual protocols are provided therein.

45. The following assays from those TGs have been characterised and included in the 2o3 DA.

- KE1 (Amino acid Derivative Reactivity Assay (ADRA), Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (DPRA); TG 442C) (2): Skin sensitisers are generally electrophilic and react with the nucleophilic moieties of proteins. The ADRA and DPRA measure depletion of two peptides/amino acids containing either cysteine or lysine residues due to covalent binding. Test protocols and prediction models are found in the TG. If borderline results are obtained for peptide depletion, additional testing should be conducted, as specified in OECD TG 442C and in **Annex 1**.

- KE2 (EpiSensA, LuSens, KeratinoSens™; TG 442D) (3): Activation of keratinocytes is demonstrated via either a) reaction of human, immortalized keratinocytes harbouring a reporter gene construct via the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway (KeratinoSens, LuSens), or b) quantification of changes in the expression of marker genes associated with keratinocyte activation in a reconstructed human epidermis model (EpiSensA). Test protocols and prediction models are found in the TG. If borderline results are obtained for luciferase induction, additional testing should be conducted, as specified in **Annex 1**.

- KE3 (Genomic Allergen Rapid Detection test (GARD®Skin), Human Cell Line Activation Test (h-CLAT), Interleukin-8 Reporter Gene Assay (IL-8 Luc), U937 Cell Line Activation Test (U-SENS); TG 442E) (4): Activation of antigen presenting cells is characterised by either a) a change in the expression of cell surface marker(s) associated with the process of activation of monocytes and DC following exposure to sensitisers (e.g. CD86, CD54; h-CLAT, U-SENS),b) changes in IL-8 expression, a cytokine associated with the activation of DC (IL-8 Luc) or c) an end-point specific genomic biomarker signature (GARD®skin). Test protocols and prediction models are found in the TG. If borderline results are obtained for the assays, additional testing should be conducted, as specified in **Annex 1**.

- In case the result of one of the 2o3 DA test methods falls into the respective test method’s BR, a 2o3 DA prediction can still be made if the outcomes of the other two test methods composing the 2o3 DA are concordant and have high confidence (i.e., results falling outside of the respective BRs).

- Similarly, in case a result is obtained in one test method that is considered inconclusive due to limitations or the chemical falling outside the applicability domain of the method, a 2o3 DA prediction can still be made if the outcomes of the other two test methods composing the 2o3 DA are concordant and have high confidence (i.e., results falling outside of the respective BRs).

- However, if the result of one of the 2o3 DA test methods falls into the respective test method’s BR or limitation, and the other two methods composing the 2o3 do not provide concordant and high confidence results, the 2o3 DA prediction is considered ‘inconclusive’. These inconclusive predictions may nevertheless be considered in a weight-of-evidence approach and/or within the context of an IATA together with other information sources. Depending on the intended use, including regulatory context, results in the borderline range above the decision threshold of the prediction model might still be considered positive; in this case, two positive outcomes can lead to an overall positive (sensitiser) prediction.

DA predictions with low confidence are considered inconclusive for hazard identification. These ‘inconclusive’ predictions may nevertheless be considered in a weight-of-evidence approach and/or within the context of an IATA together with other information sources.

# **Part II SECTION 3 - Defined Approaches for Skin Sensitisation Potency Categorisation**

## **3.1 “Integrated Testing Strategy (ITS)” Defined Approach**

### **_3.1.1 Summary_**

63. This defined approach was constructed as an Integrated Testing Strategy (ITS) for prediction of the skin sensitisation hazard potential and potency sub-categorisation according to the UN GHS (sub-categories 1A and 1B) classification system.

64. The ITS DA uses test methods that address key events (KEs) 1 and 3 in the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) leading to skin sensitisation as: KE1: protein binding (TG 442C) (2); KE3: dendritic cell activation formally described by the OECD (TG 442E) (3) and includes an _in silico_ prediction of skin sensitisation. The _in silico_ prediction of skin sensitisation is provided by Derek Nexus (4) or the OECD QSAR Toolbox (5).

65. The DIP requires a score to be calculated from each information source based on the assay-specific thresholds and the total score used to determine the final classification (**Table 3.1**). the classic ITS DA was evaluated for hazard and potency identification against a curated dataset of up to 168 chemicals with LLNA-based reference data and up to 66 chemicals with human-based reference data as found in **Annex 2** of the _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). The performance of the DAs containing alternate information sources was evaluated against between 72-159 and 37-64 substances with both LLNA-based and human-based reference data, respectively. When compared against the LLNA- and human-based reference data for hazard, the ITS DA demonstrated balanced accuracies in the range of 78-88% and 67-76%, comparable to the classic ITS DA (see **Table 3.2** and **Table 3.4**). The ITS DA demonstrated accuracies in the range of 66-80% when evaluated against LLNA-based potency data (see **Table 3.3**). When evaluated against the human data for potency, the ITS DA demonstrated comparable accuracy to the LLNA, with a range of 54-75% (vs 60% for the LLNA) (see **Table 3.5** and **3.7**). When compared against human-based reference data, 8/10 (80%) ITS permutations have equivalent or better accuracy than the LLNA. (60%). Two ITS permutations (**Table 1.3**) had lower accuracy than the LLNA when compared to human-based reference data which was due to several underpredictions of 1A sensitisers as 1B sensitisers. These underpredictions may be attributed to several considerations, including human-based sub-categorizations derived from human data where the predicted sensitising dose was close to the threshold between GHS 1A and GHS 1B sub-categorizations (termed GHS 1A-) in **Annex 4** of the _Supporting document to the OECD Guideline 497 on Defined Approaches for Skin Sensitisation_ for more information on how this was derived). This lower accuracy is specific to these ITS DA permutations only and should be taken into consideration when selecting specific DAs to use; selection of these specific DAs needs to be justified. These underprediction cases are discussed further in Annex 9 of the Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation (1).

### **_3.1.2 Data interpretation procedure_**

66. The ITS DIP uses scores assigned to the quantitative results from a KE3 (3) and a KE1 assay (1), and from an _in silico_ tool to discriminate chemicals into UN GHS category 1A (strong sensitiser); category 1B (other sensitiser), or Not Classified (non-sensitiser) (**Table 3.1**).

67. The DIP was amended from the original published version of the ITS (6-7) to change the cut-off for 1A sensitisers from a score of 7 to a score of 6 to optimize the ability of the DA to detect strong sensitisers and to extend the applicability of the ITS to chemicals for which _in silico_ predictions cannot be generated. The DIP was also altered from the published version in that it was originally applied to ECETOC categories<sup>13</sup> , and is here applied to the UN GHS subcategories.

68. The quantitative results of the KE1 and KE3 assays are converted into a score from 0 to 3 for each assay, as shown in **Table 3.1**. For the _in silico_ prediction, a positive outcome is assigned a score of 1; a negative outcome is assigned a score of 0. Further details on the respective protocols are available in **Annexes 1** and **2** and **Appendices I-II**). When these scores have been assessed, a total battery score ranging from 0 to 7, calculated by summing the individual scores, is used to predict the sensitising potential (hazard identification; UN GHS Cat. 1 vs. UN GHS NC) and potency (UN GHS Cat. 1A, Cat. 1B and NC). The positive criteria for identifying skin sensitisers (UN GHS Cat. 1) are set as a total battery score of 2 or greater. Based on the updated DIP, a total battery score is assigned into three ranks: score of 6-7 is defined as a strong (UN GHS Cat. 1A) sensitiser; score of 2-5 as moderate/weak (UN GHS Cat. 1B) sensitiser; score of 1 or 0, as not classified (_i.e._ a non-sensitiser).

## **4.1 “SARA-ICE” Defined Approach for derivation of a point of departure**

### **_4.1.1. Summary_**

87. The Skin Allergy Risk Assessment-Integrated Chemical Environment (SARA-ICE) DA is a Bayesian statistical model which estimates an ED01, which is provided as a probability distribution. The ED01 is an estimate of a dermal dose (µg cm<sup>-2</sup> ) at which there is a 1% chance of inducing sensitization from a human predictive patch test (HPPT). The SARA-ICE DA uses a set of defined input data and specifies a single point, computed as the geometric mean of the ED01 distribution conditional on it being less than 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup> , which provides a point of departure (PoD) that can be applied within a quantitative risk assessment for skin sensitisation.

88. The SARA-ICE DA is based on data from test methods from KEs 1-4 of the adverse outcome in the AOP.  The SARA-ICE DA is based on a previously published SARA model which initially included 81 chemicals in the database and was subsequently expanded to 434 chemicals, leveraging chemicals contained within the Integrated Chemical Environment (ICE, https://ice.ntp.niehs.nih.gov/, (2,3)). Further description of the database on which SARA-ICE is trained is provided in **Annex 10** of _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). Evaluation of SARA-ICE is described in paragraph 100 and performance is described in section 4.1.4. Test methods which can be used as input information sources include: the Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (DPRA; OECD TG 442C ), kinetic DPRA (kDPRA; OECD TG 442C), KeratinoSens (OECD TG 442D), the human cell line activation test (h-CLAT; OECD TG 442E), and U-SENS (OECD TG 442E), as well as _in vivo_ methods such as the local lymph node assay (LLNA; OECD TG 429, OECD TG 442A, OECD TG 442B) and the human predictive patch test (HPPT) methods i.e., human repeat insult patch tests (HRIPT) and human maximization tests (HMT) (4–10). Data in any combination from these test methods can also be used to predict the ED01. Note that the SARA-ICE model may accept existing LLNA data and HPPT data as inputs; however, new _in vivo_ data should not be generated. The SARA-ICE model should be run using a minimum of two _in chemico_ and/or _in vitro_ information sources based on different KEs.

89. The SARA-ICE DA was evaluated first using posterior predictive checks, which are recommended for any Bayesian statistical model. Checks included a comparison of prior marginal distributions against posterior marginals to ensure model parameters are identifiable from the data. The SARA-ICE DA uses latent variables to represent average quantities for each input type. Checks that latent variables are both correlated with the ED01 and their respective input data in the SARA-ICE database were performed. Generating ED01 estimates for chemicals not within the core SARA-ICE database requires the joint posterior for the full database be approximated analytically. This approximation is then used as a prior within a SARA-ICE production DA to generate ED01 estimates for new input data. Further checks were performed to ensure the production version returns the correct estimates. Details of these analyses can be found in **Annex 11** of _Supporting document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ (1). The database on which the model was evaluated is static (finalized December 2022) and in case of subsequent version changes, it will be evaluated per OECD procedures.

### **_4.1.2. Data Interpretation Procedure_**

90. The SARA-ICE DA uses a Bayesian statistical model to estimate the ED01. A PoD is computed as the geometric mean of the ED01 distribution conditional on it being less than 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup> . Technically, the model can be run with no chemical-specific inputs, in which case the resulting ED01 estimates will be taken directly from the prior distribution. Using chemical-specific data to inform estimates will, for any typical input, return an ED01 estimate whose variance (uncertainty) is reduced relative to the prior. The magnitude of reduction in uncertainty is a function of several factors including input type(s), input numbers, and input concordance. To ensure an appreciable level of uncertainty reduction is achieved, a **minimum of 2 test methods per compound**, covering 2 separate KEs from within OECD TG 442C, 442D, or 442E, should be entered into the input template used to run the model (LINK TO SARA-ICE APP<sup>16</sup>). Admissible data types are indicated in **Table 4.1** and Paragraph 88**.** There is no restriction to the maximum number of stated data inputs, in type or experimental repeats, the model can include. Any available _in vivo_ data may also be included, but new _in vivo_ data should not be produced to be used in this DA. **Figure 4.1** demonstrates the workflow for use of SARA-ICE, and further details are provided in **Appendix III** of this document.

## **_4.1.3. Description and limitations of the predictive model and its information sources_**

92. The individual _in chemico_ , _in vitro_ , and _in vivo_ information is generated using existing OECD test guidelines (OECD TG 429, 442A, 442B, 442C, 442D, 442E [4-10]) and the protocols detailed therein. In the case of the kDPRA and KeratinoSens assays, commonly reported units are expressed as molar concentrations. If these are provided as inputs, the model also requires the specification of the chemical’s molecular weight to enable conversion to mass concentrations. Note that the SARA-ICE model may accept existing LLNA data and HPPT data as inputs; however, new _in vivo_ data should not be generated.

93. The following assays have been characterized and included as potential input information sources in the SARA-ICE DA:

- Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (DPRA; OECD TG 442C; KE1 [7]): Skin sensitisers are generally electrophilic and react with the nucleophilic moieties of proteins. The DPRA measures depletion of two peptides containing either cysteine or lysine residues due to covalent binding. A test chemical that induces mean peptide depletion of cysteine- and lysine-containing peptide above 6.38% (or in the case of co-elution, cysteine-only depletion above 13.89%) is considered to be positive. The SARA-ICE DA does not make use of this classification rule. Instead, the input into SARA-ICE is the percentage depletion of both peptides. The SARA-ICE DA then determines the maximum depletion of either of the peptides. Depletions less than 1% are interpreted as < 1%, whilst depletions greater than 99% are interpreted as > 99%. It is noted that while the DPRA gravimetric method has not been specifically evaluated for use in the SARA-ICE DA, given that the DPRA gravimetric evaluation that was conducted under OECD TG 442C found that the approaches are equivalent, it is expected to provide data within an acceptable range for use as an input into SARA-ICE.

- Kinetic Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (kDPRA; OECD TG 442C; KE1 [7]): The kDPRA is intended to quantify the reactivity of test chemicals in a time and concentration dependent manner. The rate of depletion of the cysteine peptide (used within the DPRA) is determined. Kinetic rate constants are calculated and reported as the log Kmax value with Kmax having units M<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. The kDPRA is used in the discrimination of UN GHS subcategory 1A from those not categorized as sub-category 1A. For SARA-ICE, the log Kmax is converted to units g L<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> and multiplied by -1 (such that higher potency chemicals are associated with lower values). This is handled by the model, assuming the molecular weight of the chemical has been specified. If the reactivity rate is too slow to measure, then the input should be < X where X is the limit of detection of the test. For training purposes, this has been assumed to be -3.5 M<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>, thus, for chemicals exhibiting negligible reactivity in the kDPRA, the input should be <-3.5.

- KeratinoSens: (In vitro Skin Sensitisation: ARE-Nrf2 Luciferase Test Method; OECD TG 442D; KE2 [6]): Keratinocytes harbouring a reporter gene construct react to possible sensitisers via the Nrf2Keap1 pathway. A test chemical that causes > 1.5 fold luciferase induction, at viabilities > 70% when compared to the vehicle control, is considered to be positive. The SARA-ICE input is the EC1.5 (µM) and the IC50 (µM). These are converted by the template to units of µg mL<sup>-1</sup>, assuming molecular weight has been provided. The model also accepts inputs as > X or < X where X is a test concentration within the assay. This is required, for example, to input a negative test into the model (for which no EC1.5 is defined). If the test is negative, then no luciferase induction occurs at any test concentration and the input into the model should > X where X is the maximum tested concentration. Similarly, if > 1.5 fold induction is observed at all test concentrations (i.e., the test is positive at all test concentrations), the input into SARA-ICE should be provided as < X where X is the minimum test concentration.

- Human Cell Line Activation Test (h-CLAT; OECD TG442E; KE3 [4]): Activation of antigen presenting cells is characterized by the up-regulation of CD86 and/or CD54. The h-CLAT is considered to be positive if CD86 induction exceeds 1.5 fold and/or CD54 exceeds 2 fold at viabilities > 50% when compared to the vehicle control. The SARA-ICE input is both the CD54 EC200 and the CD86 EC150 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>) and the CV75 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>). If either value is not reported because no change in the biomarker was observed, the input is > X where X is the maximum concentration tested. In the case that induction exceeds thresholds at all test concentrations for either biomarker (i.e., the test is positive for that biomarker at all test concentrations), the model may also accept < X where X is the minimum test concentration.

- U-SENS (OECD TG 442E; KE3 [4]): Activation of antigen presenting cells is characterised by the up-regulation of CD86. The U-SENS is considered positive if the CD86 induction exceeds 1.5-fold at viabilities > 70% compared to vehicle control. The SARA-ICE input is the CD86 EC150 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>) and CV70 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>). If the test is negative, the input to the model is > X where X is the maximum concentration should be used. In the event that CD86 induction exceeds the 1.5-fold at all test concentrations, the input should be < X where X is the minimum test concentration.

- Local Lymph Node Assay (LLNA; OECD TG429, 442A, 442B; KE4 [5,8,9]): The induction phase of skin sensitization (KE4) is characterized by lymphocyte proliferation in the lymph nodes draining the site of the exposure and is proportional to the dose and potency of the substance. The LLNA measures proliferation relative to the vehicle control treated group for mice exposed on their ear to a test substance of interest. The ratio of the mean proliferation for each treatment group compared to the vehicle control is the Stimulation Index (SI). An SI ≥ 3 is considered to be indicative of skin sensitization. The input for SARA-ICE template is the EC3 expressed as a percent. These are converted to µg cm<sup>-2</sup> by the model. The model may also accept an input for negative tests expressed as > X where X is the maximum tested concentration. Alternatively, if all test concentrations result in an SI greater than 3, the recommended input into SARA-ICE is < X where X is the lowest concentration tested. This also allows single-dose LLNA studies to be input in the SARA-ICE DA. If a single-dose LLNA is run at concentration X and the SI < 3, then the input into SARA-ICE is > X. Conversely, if the SI > 3, the study result can be entered as < X. Only existing LLNA data are recommended for use in the SARA-ICE model.

- Human Predictive Patch Test (HPPT [10]): HPPT methods include the human maximization test (HMT) and the human repeat insult patch test (HRIPT). Both test methods quantify an empirical sensitization rate following exposure to a (potential) allergen at some chosen dermal dose. The HMT incorporates a pre-treatment with an irritant and typically has a smaller test pool than HRIPT. Required HPPT inputs into the SARA-ICE DA are the dermal dose expressed as µg cm<sup>-2</sup>, the total number of test subjects and the total number of subjects sensitized following the challenge.

94. The current limitations of the individual test methods, such as with respect to solubility, are described in the respective test guidelines and the validation studies cited therein. No filtering of assay data was performed prior to training the SARA-ICE DA, therefore uncertainty measures provided by the DA should reflect such limitations. However, caution is advised when selecting inputs since biases may nevertheless occur (e.g., when using a DPRA input for a chemical whose sensitization potential is enhanced via metabolic activation). Only valid assay results, i.e. results within the applicability domain of each assay used as input, should be used to run the DA for regulatory purposes. Evaluation of data quality should be made prior to use in the DA, this includes use of human data for which Strickland et al., 2023 [10] can be used for reference.

## **_4.1.4. Confidence in the SARA-ICE DA predictions_**

95. The SARA-ICE DA is built upon a Bayesian statistical model and as such provides potency estimates with a numerical measure of precision. The provided distribution represents the range of ED01 values that are both consistent with user-specified input data and the model – where the model is a characterization of the association between ED01 estimates and assay data for a training set of 434 chemicals. Further information on the SARA-ICE database can be found in the Technical Specification in Appendix 2. A decision on whether to use specific source data is dictated by the limitations of the different input methods found within the Annex of the requisite test method (TG 429, TG 442A, TG 442B, TG 442C, TG 442D, TG 442E). As indicated, for a higher confidence estimate, at least 2 methods that cover 2 key events should be used as input data. A greater number of data inputs will likely also increase confidence (e.g. decrease the width of the distribution) of the model ED01 output depending on the concordance of the additional data utilized [3]. All available high-quality data is encouraged to be used.

96. A number of basic model checks were carried out to ensure that the SARA-ICE model is behaving as expected. Posterior predictive checks show that the marginal posterior distribution of each parameter is not concentrating in the extreme tail of the prior, indicating that the chosen prior is only weakly informative on the posterior estimate. Therefore, estimates of model parameters would be unlikely to change if prior choices were relaxed. Further checks were completed to confirm the model assumption that there is a linear relationship with non-zero slope to the predictors included in the model. Figure 4.2 shows that each of the possible inputs is informative towards estimation of the ED01. Further analysis shows that location-like latent variables for each predictor are both correlated with the ED01 and experimental repeats for chemical. The SARA-ICE Technical Specification in **Annex 10** of the _Supporting Document to the Guideline (GL) on Defined Approaches (DAs) for Skin Sensitisation_ [1] provides a detailed explanation of the basic model checks carried out.

## **_4.1.6. Predictive Capacity of the SARA-ICE DA_**

98. SARA-ICE derives a point of departure for risk assessment. The model estimates the dose at which skin sensitisation is likely to occur in 1% of an HPPT-eligible human population (ED01), reported as a distribution with associated confidence intervals. For PoD estimation, an upper bound is imposed on the ED01 distribution at a threshold of 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup>. This reflects the bound for a GHS classification of NC and encodes an implicit assumption that the chemical under consideration for PoD based risk assessment is a sensitiser. The PoD is therefore defined as the geometric mean of the distribution of the ED01 conditional on the ED01 being less than 60,000 µg cm<sup>-2</sup>.

99. PoDs were computed for all 196 chemicals in the DASS evaluation set (Annex 2) using a) _in chemico_ and _in vitro_ data also reported in the evaluation set (which consists of a single input for DPRA, KeratinoSens and h-CLAT) and b) a single kDPRA sourced from Natsch et al., 2022 [12], if available). It should be noted that 187 / 196 (95%) of chemicals in the DASS evaluation set are presented in the SARA-ICE database. However, the data used for training versus evaluation differs for most chemicals. For training purposes, a chemical may have both _in vivo_ and _in vitro_ data with multiple studies per data type. For evaluation, inputs are restricted to a single DPRA, single KeratinoSens, single h-CLAT and, if available, a single kDPRA study. It follows that ED01 estimates obtained during training may be considerably different from those obtained in the evaluation.

100. SARA-ICE PoDs, were compared to reference DSA1+ values reported in Herzler et al., 2024 [11]. The reference value is termed the _median sensitization potency estimate_ (MSPE) and is reported as weight-of-evidence potency metric calculated from human data. A DSA1+ MSPE value was defined for 54 / 196 chemicals in the DASS evaluation set. A positive correlation exists (Pearson correlation coefficient is 0.64) and 46 / 54 (85%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value. Roughly one third, 19 / 54 (35%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. The same SARA-ICE PoDs were also compared to 154 chemicals with reference LLNA MLLPs sourced from OECD DASS reference list [1]. A positive correlation exists (Pearson correlation coefficient is 0.71) and 122 / 154 (79%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value. Again, roughly one third, 49 / 154 (32%) of SARA-ICE PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. ## **4.2 “Regression-based” Defined Approach for derivation of a point of departure for skin sensitization**

### **_4.2.1 Summary_**

102. The regression-based defined approach (DA) is based on multiple linear regression models. As input data, quantitative (dose-response and kinetic) data from _in vitro_ and _in chemico_ assays addressing KE1, KE2 and KE3 of the AOP are used. Different models were trained on (i) EC3 values from the Local Lymph Node Assay (LLNA) [1, 2] and (ii) on weight-of-evidence (WoE) Potency Values (PV)[3] derived from human DSA04 and NOEL values and LLNA EC3 values [4]. These individual regression models are combined in the DA to provide a finite point of departure (PoD) that can be applied within a quantitative risk assessment for skin sensitisation [5]. The PoD is both expressed as a value in % in a vehicle applied at 25 mg/cm<sup>2</sup> to the skin (similar to the LLNA) or as a dose per unit metric in µg/cm<sup>2</sup>. The conversion factor between these two metrics is a fixed factor of 250.

103. The details of the training of the regression models and the set of chemicals used are described in detail in the open access literature [1-3]. The database with all the input data and the prediction of the PoD for all chemicals tested (n = 220) is provided in **Annex 12.a** of Supporting Document to this document. The specific test methods in the KE-based test guidelines which can be used as input information sources include: the kinetic DPRA (kDPRA; OECD TG 442C), KeratinoSens (KS; OECD TG 442D) and the human cell line activation test (h-CLAT; OECD TG 442E) [6-8]. Data from at least two of these information sources are required to predict the PoD. For chemicals, which are rated as sensitisers according to a DA described in Part I or Part II of this guideline, the input data shall include quantitative results from at least two positive KE outcomes, whereby the kDPRA is conducted as a quantitative test for KE1 in case of a positive DPRA. For chemicals positive in DPRA and KS, data from at least kDPRA and KS shall be used. For chemicals positive in DPRA and h-CLAT, data from at least kDPRA and h-CLAT shall be used.  For chemicals positive in KS and h-CLAT, at least these data shall be used. For chemicals tested in all three KE tests and positive in at least two tests, all available evidence including the kDPRA result shall be used, see Figure 4.3. In addition, vapour pressure is used to correct for volatility under topical application. Evaluation of predictivity of the DA is described in section 4.2.6.

### **_4.2.2 Data Interpretation Procedure_**

The models combine these data in linear regression equations with coefficients trained on the full dataset. These calculations are made in a fully transparent spreadsheet which is provided as **Annex 12.b** of the Supporting Document to this guideline [11]. The measured parameters summarised in Table 4.2 can be directly entered into the spreadsheet to arrive at a conclusion. Depending on the available input data, multiple predictions are made: (i) Equations EQ1, EQ4, EQ5 and EQ6 were trained against LLNA data (see[1]), while (ii) EQ1d, EQ4d, EQ6d and EQ5e were trained against the PV (see [3]). The DA then automatically selects, depending on the KE covered by the input data, a PoD according to the workflow in Figure 4.3 below, taking the more conservative estimate from the two models trained on either LLNA EC3 or PV values.

Appropriate safety factors, depending on the use situation, shall be applied to this PoD in quantitative risk assessment, which is not within the scope of this guideline.

## **_4.2.3 Description and limitations of the predictive model and its information sources_**

106. The individual _in chemico_ , _in vitro_ , and _in vivo_ information is generated using existing OECD test guidelines (OECD TG 442C, 442D, 442E  [6-8].) and the protocols detailed therein. In the case of the kDPRA and the KeratinoSens assays, commonly reported units are expressed as molar concentrations. The model requires the specification of the chemical’s molecular weight to enable conversion to mass concentrations. In case the molecular weight is unknown (mixtures and multi-constituent substances of unknown composition (i.e. UVCB substances of unknown or variable composition, complex reaction products or biological materials), a default molecular weight of 200 g/mol can be used as applied in the gravimetric DPRA (442C). This default molecular weight is also used in the gravimetric KeratinoSens prediction model (442D). The regression-based DA can be run using data from a minimum of two KE-based _in chemico_ and/or _in vitro_ information sources applicable to a particular chemical. Applicability domain limitations as outlined in TG442c, 442d and 442e for a particular chemical may guide which assay is being conducted. If a chemical is rated as a sensitizer due to at least two positive test results in TG 442C, 442D, 442E, at least the data from two positive KE shall be used whereby the kDPRA is conducted as a quantitative test for KE1 in case of a positive DPRA. For chemicals positive in DPRA and KS, data from at least kDPRA and KS shall be used. For chemicals positive in DPRA and h-CLAT, data from at least kDPRA and h-CLAT shall be used.  For chemicals positive in KS and h-CLAT, at least these data shall be used. For chemicals tested in all three KE tests and positive in at least two tests, all available evidence including the kDPRA result shall be used, see Figure 4.3. For chemicals for which the kDPRA is not applicable, potency is assessed by a combination of KS and h-Clat. For details on the underlying rationale and for possible testing schemes see [2]. All the test methods used for the regression DA are technically applicable to test mixtures and considerations of the different test methods when using a gravimetric approach should be followed. When assessing mixtures and no experimental vapor pressure is known, the vapor pressure is set to zero. This is a conservative approach as the actual vapor pressure can only reduce the predicted sensitization potency. The models have been trained on the three specific KE-based tests and using large sets of dose-response data. If for other KE-based tests such quantitative datasets are available, similar regression-based models may be derived in the future in analogy to [1, 2]. However, these would need completely independent validation and assessment for predictivity and the development of an independent calculation worksheet. Therefore, currently the DA is only applicable to the prescribed methods.

107. The following assays have been characterized and included as input information sources in the regression-based DA:

- Kinetic Direct Peptide Reactivity Assay (kDPRA; OECD TG 442C; KE1 [7]): The kDPRA is intended to quantify the reactivity of test chemicals in a time and concentration dependent manner. The rate of depletion of the cysteine peptide (used within the DPRA) is determined. Kinetic rate constants are calculated and reported as the Log kmax value with kmax having units M<sup>-1</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>. For chemicals with no measurable reactivity above the threshold in the kDPRA, the limit vale of the Log kmax = -3.5 is entered into the model as specified in the Spreadsheet in **Annex 12.b** of Supporting Document to this guideline [11]. For chemicals with a high reactivity at the earliest time point measured (10 min in the standard setting of the kDPRA), testing at shorter incubation times should be considered in order to not underestimate potency (as indicated in TG442C, ANNEX III, para 18).

- KeratinoSens: (In vitro Skin Sensitisation: ARE-Nrf2 Luciferase Test Method; OECD TG 442D; KE2[6]): Keratinocytes harbouring a reporter gene construct react to possible sensitisers via the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway. A test chemical that causes > 1.5-fold luciferase induction, at viabilities > 70% when compared to the vehicle control, is considered to be positive. The model input is the EC1.5 (µM) and the IC50 (µM). For chemicals with no defined molecular weight, these can be entered in µg mL<sup>-1</sup>. If no luciferase induction is observed at any test concentration (i.e., the test is negative), the input into the model should be 4000 µM (or 800 µg mL<sup>-1</sup>), i.e. twice the maximal concentration tested. The same approach applies to the IC50 value in case no cytotoxicity is observed as specified in the Spreadsheet in **Annex 12.b** of Supporting Document to this guideline [11].

- Human Cell Line Activation Test (h-CLAT; OECD TG442E; KE3 [8]): Activation of antigen presenting cells is characterized by the up-regulation of CD86 and/or CD54. The h-CLAT is considered to be positive if CD86 induction exceeds 1.5-fold and/or CD54 exceeds 2-fold at viabilities > 50% when compared to the vehicle control. The model input is both the CD54 EC200 and the CD86 EC150 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>) and the CV75 (µg mL<sup>-1</sup>). If either value is not reported because no change in the biomarker was observed or cytotoxicity is observed at the maximal test concentration, the input is 5000 µg mL<sup>-1</sup>.

108. If multiple test results are available for a given test, they are assessed for their validity based on the criteria laid out in the KE-based test guidelines. If multiple valid and concordant results are available, the geometric mean of the individual parameters from multiple tests is used in the calculation spreadsheet. In cases where multiple positive and negative results are available for a given KE-event based test, the geometric mean of the data from the positive tests is used.

109. The current limitations of individual _in chemico_ and _in vitro_ test methods, such as limitations with respect to solubility, are described in the respective test guidelines (TG 442C, Appendix 1; TG 442D, Appendix 1A; TG 442E, Annex 1) and the validation studies cited therein (2, 3, 4). No filtering of assay data to exclude chemicals outside of the application domain was performed prior to training the regression models, therefore uncertainty measures provided by the DA should reflect such limitations. Specifically, (i) data for chemicals falling into the borderline range of the individual methods were still used and (ii) h-CLAT data were used independently of the cLogP of the test chemical when the models were trained. However, caution is advised when selecting inputs since biases may nevertheless occur (e.g., when using a kDPRA input for a chemical whose sensitisation potential is enhanced via metabolic or abiotic activation, or for using the kDPRA for chemicals which are predominantly reacting with amine groups as shown e.g. by the reaction with the Lysine peptide in the DPRA; limitations of the kDPRA are summarised in TG442C, APPENDIX III, ANNEX 1). Therefore, only valid assay results, i.e. results within the applicability domain of each assay, should be used to run the DA for regulatory purposes. Evaluation of data quality should be made prior to use in the DA.

## **_4.2.4 Confidence in the regression-based DA predictions_**

110. The regression equations were built upon a training set of n = 139 - 203 chemicals. Further information on the underlying database can be found in the **Annex 12.b** and in [1-3]. A decision on whether to use specific source data is dictated by the limitations of the different input methods found within the Annex of the respective test method (TG 442C, TG 442D and TG 442E). At least 2 methods that cover 2 key events should be used as input data.

111. The correlation of individual information sources and their combination to sensitiser potency in the LLNA has been analysed in the literature [9]. The stability of the regression models on different datasets have been analysed in detail, indicating a high robustness of the regression-based models [1, 2]. The consistency of the result when using three or only two information sources was investigated in supporting file ESM3 to [1], which is accessible under 10.14573/altex.2201141. The performance of the individual regression models was shown as summary statistics in Table 2 in [1] and in Table 4 in [3], and for individual chemicals in the file ESM1 to [1] accessible under 10.14573/altex.2201141.  A high consistency of predictions was also observed if the models were trained on LLNA EC3 only or on PV, with a lower importance of cytotoxicity and a higher contribution of cellular activation markers for models trained on PV [3] (see Table 4 in [3]). The uncertainty in predicting _in vivo_ data using these models should also be viewed in the context of the inherent variability of human and animal reference data and the correlation between these data, see e.g. [10].

## **_4.2.6 Predictive Capacity of the regression-based DA_**

113. The regression-based DA derives a PoD for risk assessment. The model estimates the dose of the EC3 value in the LLNA or the dose at which skin sensitisation is likely to occur in 4% of a HPPT-eligible human population (DSA04 value). DSA04 value in µg/cm<sup>2</sup> are correlated to EC3 values expressed in µg/cm<sup>2</sup> and both values represent the lower end of the dose-response curve for experimental induction of skin sensitization. These values are combined in the PV [3, 4]. The PoD is therefore defined as a best estimate for either the LLNA EC3 or the WoE PV. PoDs were computed for 220 chemicals listed in Annex 4.2.A. The PoDs were compared to reference EC3 values or PV. When evaluating the PoD vs. the LLNA EC3 MLLP in the DASS database (n=146), 126/146 (86%) of regression-based PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value, with 8/146 (5.5%) having a > 10-fold underpredicted potency. Furthermore, 59/146 (40%) of the PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. When the PoD are compared to the LLNA EC3 in the full database (n=220; including those EC3 values non-evaluated or excluded by the DASS review), a similar result is obtained: 191/220 (87%) of regression-based PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value, with 13/220 (6.3%) having a > 10-fold underpredicted potency. Furthermore, 80/220 (36%) of the PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. The same PoDs were compared to the PV (n=139). 120/139 (86%) of PoDs are within 10-fold of the reference value, with 12/139 (8.9%) having a > 10-fold underpredicted potency. Furthermore, 43/139 (31%) of PoDs are within 2-fold of the reference value. Regression-based PoDs give a balanced prediction relative to the chosen reference values (geometric mean fold-differences vs. LLNA is 0.86 and vs. PV it is 0.99). Further analyses are made in **Annex 12.c** in the Supporting Document of this guideline [11]. # **Annex 2. Assessing confidence in DASS ITS predictions**

## **Introduction**

116. As described in **Section 3.1,** the ITS defined approach (DA) are based on three information sources: an _in chemico_ assay (DPRA, ADRA, TG 442C) (1), an _in vitro_ assay (h-CLAT, U-SENS™, GARD®Skin, TG 442E) (2), and one _in silico_ method (Derek Nexus or OECD Toolbox) prediction from the models listed in Appendices I-II of this guideline (referred to hereafter as _in silico_)) (3-4). For each information source a score is given depending on the outcome of the individual assay and/or prediction, that is then summed to obtain the DA prediction (5).

## **Applicability domain of the individual information sources**

### **_In chemico/in vitro information source_**

117. A test chemical is considered to be within the _in chemico_ / _in vitro_ information source domain (i.e. applicable) if it can be tested according to the individual protocols, taking into account the technical and chemical type limitations of each assay (as defined in the respective TGs (442C and 442E (1, 2)). The _in chemico_ / _in vitro_ results are considered applicable, in case there are no technical or chemical space specific limitations and no reason why the results obtained from the assay cannot be considered.

### **_In silico information source_**

118. The ITS DAs use _in silico_ information sources that are based on chemical structures. These _in silico_ sources rely on molecular representation of the chemicals: input usually by drawing the chemical structure, or by entering the Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) or the IUPAC International Chemical Identifier (InChi). A test chemical is considered to be within the _in silico_ information source domain if it is a single chemical (i.e. mixtures and UVCBs are outside the applicability domain) and a prediction can be made, taking into account the individual applicability domain of the _in silico_ model (as defined in Appendix I-II). As a single chemical can be represented by several CAS or EC numbers (due to differences in composition, e.g. stereochemical differences, present as varied salt forms, present as the main component in a mixture), it is important to specify the exact structure. Resources such as the US EPA CompTox Chemicals Dashboard (https://comptox.epa.gov/dashboard) or NIH PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) may be useful in mapping chemical names or structures to SMILES or InChi format. Available guidance can be consulted regarding minimum purity level of substances used in _in silico_ predictions based on molecular structure.<sup>1718</sup>

## **Confidence in ITS predictions**

119. The applicability domain of the individual information sources used in the ITS DA are assessed and this determines whether the ITS predictions can be considered conclusive (i.e. high confidence) or inconclusive (i.e. low confidence) for hazard identification and/or potency.

### **_How to apply the data interpretation procedure (DIP) for the ITS_**

120. The ITS uses three information sources based on _in chemico_ (KE1) (1) and _in vitro_ (KE3) data (2), and an _in silico_ prediction (3), with a DIP developed with EG DASS input. Where all three information sources are applicable, a conclusive ITS prediction can be made. In some cases, a conclusive ITS prediction can be made, if there are only two information sources with applicable results ( **Figure A2.1** ).

# **Appendix I: Description, applicability domain, and protocol for Derek Nexus predictions**

### **Description of Derek Nexus**

125. Derek is an expert, knowledge-based software tool comprising alerts on several toxicity endpoints, including skin sensitisation. Derek fires alerts based on structural features, _i.e._ whether a hapten has potential for electrophilic binding to skin proteins either directly or following metabolism/auto-oxidation (1). Additional information on this model can be found in the QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) (2).

### **Applicability domain of Derek Nexus**

126. To each skin sensitisation alert, a likelihood level is associated. Chemicals firing an alert with a likelihood of certain, probable, plausible, or equivocal are considered to be positive. Chemicals with a negative prediction of ‘non-sensitiser with no misclassified or unclassified features’ are considered to be negative.

127. Skin sensitisation predictions from Derek Nexus v6.1.0 are used in the ITS DA. All positive predictions (likelihood = certain, probable, plausible or equivocal) are considered to be inside the applicability domain. Negative predictions (likelihood = doubted, improbable, impossible or non-sensitiser) are also considered to be in the applicability domain unless they contain misclassified and/or unclassified features. A prediction of non-sensitiser with misclassified features indicates the presence of a fragment that has been observed exclusively in known sensitisers which Derek fails to alert for. A prediction of non-sensitiser with unclassified features indicates the presence of a fragment that has not been observed in publicly available data (although Derek may have seen this in proprietary data) (3). Usually, expert review is recommended for predictions containing these features but as a fixed data interpretation procedure, required in a DA, does not permit expert review these are best considered as out of domain for use in the ITS DA (**Figure 8**).

# **Appendix II: Description, applicability domain, and protocol for OECD QSAR Toolbox predictions**

### **Description of OECD QSAR Toolbox**

129. The OECD QSAR Toolbox is a freely available software application that supports reproducible and transparent chemical hazard assessment. It offers functionalities for retrieving experimental data, simulating metabolism and profiling properties of chemicals. These information and tools can be used to find structurally and mechanistically defined analogues and chemical categories, which can serve as sources for read-across and trend analysis for data gap filling (1)]. Additional information on this model can be found in the QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) (2).

### **Applicability domain of OECD QSAR Toolbox**

#### **_Technical aspects_**

130. The Toolbox prediction used by the DA ITS is calculated using the DASS automated workflow (DASS AW) included in OECD QSAR Toolbox v.4.5. The workflow also includes the automatic calculation of the applicability domain.

#### **_Calculation of the in silico domain of Toolbox_**

131. Applicability domain of the QSAR Toolbox Skin sensitisation predictions for use in the ITS defined approach automated workflow (DASS AW) is defined by based on the training set substances of the same automated workflow. The training set (TS) consists of 2268 substances having LLNA and/or GPMT skin sensitisation experimental data<sup>20</sup> (the full list of substances can be consulted in the QSAR Toolbox). The TS substances are part of the following OECD QSAR Toolbox databases:

1. Skin sensitisation;

2. REACH Skin sensitisation (normalized) databases.

132. Based on the correctly predicted training set substances, three layers of applicability domain are automatically calculated by the Toolbox: 1) parametric; 2) structural and 3) mechanistic layers. Depending on the Toolbox prediction approach (read-across or profiling predictions) and prediction outcomes (positive or negative), one or more of these layers are taken into account to establish the overall Toolbox domain of the specific prediction.

> 20 In case of multiple data points for one substance, the most conservative scenario is taken into account.

134. Explanation and rationale for the use of different domain layers:

1. Positive predictions (both by read-across and profiling): the presence of an alert (which is the requirement for positive Toolbox prediction to be considered within in the mechanistic domain) is sufficient to consider the prediction to be within the Toolbox domain. Substances triggering an alert are considered as in domain because they contain the toxicophore that has been observed experimentally in skin sensitisers. No further checks are needed in this context to consider the prediction within the Toolbox _in silico_ domain.

2. Negative predictions by read-across: the structural and parametric domains are not taken into account because the Toolbox has already ensured some level of similarity with other substances in its training set that met the requirements to be selected as suitable analogues for read-across (these requirements are explained in detail in the DASS AW description).

3. Negative prediction by profiling predictions: all domain layers are taken into account to ensure the highest possible reliability level for the Toolbox prediction. Stricter requirements are needed mainly for two reasons: 1. lack of alerts is not equal to proof of lack of sensitisation potential and 2. to apply a cautious approach since acceptance of negative predictions may lower the human health protection level risk in case of a false negative predictions.

##### _2. Structural layer_

The structural layer is defined based on the atom centred fragments (ACF) derived from the structural characteristics of the TS substances that are correctly predicted<sup>22</sup> by the DASS AW.

The ACF are defined according to the following Toolbox default values for ACF:

- Any atom distance = 1

- Heteroatom distance = 1

- Extract C (sp<sup>3</sup>) fragments = YES

- Include whole aromatic rings = NO

For each substance, the following values are calculated:

- % Correct fragments: percentage of ACF occurring in correctly predicted structures in the training set

- % incorrect fragments: percentage of ACF occurring in incorrectly predicted structures in the training set

- % unknown fragments: percentage of ACF not occurring in the training set.

A substance is considered within the structural domain of the DASS AW if 100% of its ACF belong to the correct fragments.

##### _3. Mechanistic layer_

The predicted capability of a substance to interact with the skin proteins without and after (a)biotic activation is taken into consideration. A positive prediction is considered within the mechanistic domain if the substance triggers alerts from the profiler “_Protein binding for skin sensitization by OASIS_” without or after (a)biotic activation.

A negative prediction is considered within the mechanistic domain if the substance does not trigger alerts from the profiler “_Protein binding for skin sensitization by OASIS_” without or after (a)biotic activation.

### **2. Running the SARA-ICE Model**

1. Click ‘Choose input’ on the SARA-ICE DA user interface

3. Click ‘Run Analysis’

_Running the model involves drawing samples from the posterior distribution using the No-UTurn sampler implemented within the probabilistic programming language Stan. By default, a sample of 10,000 draws of the ED01 is returned to the user. Summary statistics such as quantile estimates of the distribution are calculated empirically on this sample._

_The No-U-Turn sampler produces a Markov Chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior distribution of the model. A successful model run is achieved when the Markov Chain converges to the target distribution. The Stan software provides several measures of convergence to assess whether convergence may be assumed._
